Refrain

Scope approved · revised Oct 3, 2026 AI focus & memory coach Canonical: devpost/scope.md

A short survey builds your training roadmap; during each study session your own distractions become personal rules, and after it you teach back the core idea so the coach can quiz you from memory next time.

The unique kernel

One loop trains both focus and memory — and it runs on your own words.

During a session · focus

Tap once each time you're pulled away and jot what pulled you. The coach turns those notes into one personal if-then rule.

After a session · memory

Explain the core idea in two or three sentences. The coach shows what's missing and writes two questions you answer from memory next time.

Roadmap · habit

The next session resizes to what you actually managed. No streak ever resets to zero.

The AI never summarizes the material for you: you do the remembering and the filtering; the coach checks and guides.

The “oh, that's cool” beats: two-word notes like “team chat” and “email ping” come back as a rule written for this person, the next session asks them to recall exactly what they explained — and a meeting that cuts a session short doesn't count against them.

The core loop

Step 1 of 6

Why they come back: the plan becomes more personal after every session, the warm-up shows them remembering more, session length grows from a level they can actually reach, and a bad day shrinks the next step instead of erasing progress.

Who it's for

Audience

Adults (18 and over) who want to retrain how they focus and how much they remember of what they study — university students and working adults. The survey adapts the plan to each.

Demo persona

A working adult like the learner: a junior manager who studies after work, gets pulled into meetings and urgent tasks, drifts to team chat and email in between, and forgets much of what they studied by the next week.

What they do today

Block calendar time, set a Pomodoro timer or a blocker (Forest, Opal, Freedom), then re-read notes. Blockers never learn why they got distracted, streak apps punish one missed day, a meeting that cuts a session short counts as a failure, and re-reading is one of the least effective ways to remember.

What “working” looks like — the two-minute demo

  1. Answer the four survey questions.
  2. See a personal roadmap with a starting session length and the reason for it.
  3. Run a demo-length session, tapping “Distracted” twice with notes like “team chat” and “email ping”.
  4. Explain the core idea in two sentences.
  5. The debrief shows a gap in the explanation, two questions for next time, a personal if-then rule, and the roadmap moving.
  6. The next session opens with those two questions to answer from memory — and the rule on screen.
  7. A meeting cuts that session short: “I was pulled away”, a one-line note of where they stopped — and the roadmap holds instead of easing back.

Explicitly cut — tap for the reason

Speed reading

Reading speed trades off against comprehension; doubling or tripling it without losing understanding is unlikely (Rayner et al. 2016), and one-word-at-a-time apps impair comprehension. Kept instead: lose less reading time to distraction and remember more of what you read.

AI summaries of the material

Reading a summary is re-study; retrieval practice beats re-study (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). The user explains; the coach checks.

Streaks that reset to zero

One missed day does not materially affect habit formation (Lally et al.), and streak guilt is a known complaint about apps such as Forest.

Automatic distraction detection

Needs device-level permissions; tapping it yourself proves the kernel and is itself the awareness practice.

Website and app blocking

Plenty of blockers exist; this project trains focus rather than building walls.

Brain-training games

Training happens during the user's real study or work.

Medical or ADHD claims

This is a study-habit coach, not a clinical tool.

Research map

Gathered with Tavily on Oct 3, 2026 and checked against the source pages. Blog-only statistics without traceable citations were left out.

Focus and habits
Habit formation takes time — and one miss is fine

Median 66 days to automaticity (range 18–254); missing one opportunity “did not materially affect the habit formation process.” Lally et al. 2010 · 2024 systematic review

If-then plans work

Medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). Summary

Attention on screens is short; focus can be trained step by step

≈ 47 seconds on one screen (Gloria Mark). “Focus reps” lengthened from 5–7 to 25–30 minutes (Edutopia); evidence still mixed (Harvard Health).

Breaks and logging

Fixed breaks gave better mood with the same task completion (Maastricht); a 2025 study found no overall difference (PMC). A self-monitoring log was part of an RCT that reduced smartphone distraction (Mind over Matter).

Memory and learning
Which study techniques actually work

Practice testing and distributed practice: high utility. Self-explanation: moderate. Summarization, highlighting, rereading, keyword mnemonics: low (Dunlosky et al. 2013).

Testing beats re-reading

After one week: 61% of a passage recalled with repeated testing vs 40% with repeated study (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).

Speed reading doesn't hold up

Speed–comprehension trade-off (Rayner et al. 2016); one-word-at-a-time apps do not foster comprehension (PubMed, UC San Diego).

For the Later memory drills

Six weeks of method-of-loci training substantially improved word-list recall, still present months later (Dresler et al. 2017); face-name mnemonic (Yesavage 1984); the major system for numbers, evidence mostly from practitioners (overview).

Problem size and pitfalls
The problem is real

73% of UK students aged 18–22 spend 4+ hours a day on their phone; over 3 in 4 believe it hurts their grades (Inside Higher Ed) · OECD 2024

Don't cite “phone presence drains your brain”

Ward et al. 2017 failed replication (FORRT), and a meta-analysis found limited support.

Closest existing products
What already exists — and how this differs
  • Focusito — distraction tap + AI pattern coaching
  • Thawly — AI if-then chains for starting tasks
  • FocusFlow — focus timer + 5-question AI quiz from your PDF or notes
  • WorkspaceLM — AI quizzes from uploaded content
  • AI habit coaches (Sortyd, MyOrbit) · indie “focus like a muscle” app

Difference: one loop joins distraction-to-rule focus training with teach-back recall in the user's own words, on a no-reset roadmap.

How this scope was shaped

  • Original: the learner set the theme, the audience, and the survey → AI roadmap flow with its four questions. At the learner's request, the agent researched with Tavily and proposed the daily loop and kernel; the learner approved.
  • Revision (during 3-prd): the learner added memory training and asked to go deeper than Focusito. The agent researched each example; the learner approved keeping teach-back and next-session recall in the PoC, moving memory drills to Later, and cutting speed reading.
  • Decisions during 3-prd: the learner shared their story as a junior manager, chose a working-adult demo persona like themselves, approved an End-early choice that separates being pulled away from losing focus, and chose the name Refrain from a researched, collision-checked shortlist.
  • Revision (during 5-build): the audience became adults 18 and over, because Google Cloud's generative AI terms don't allow a site directed to, or likely used by, people under 18. The survey accepts ages 18–120, and the Welcome notice says Refrain is for adults.
